1. Age-Related Pregnancy Risks

Adolescent Pregnancy

Pathophysiology
Physiologic immaturity combines with psychosocial factors (peer pressure, high sexual activity, limited/inaccurate knowledge of contraception, fear of telling parents) to raise obstetric risk.
Assessment
Higher incidence in Hispanic and Black/African American teens. Assess for late entry to (or absence of) prenatal care, anemia, and signs of dystocia or preeclampsia.
Teaching
Sex education including STI prevention; preconception counseling; realistic education on contraceptive options and correct use.
Priority
Getting the teen into prenatal care early — teens statistically present late or not at all.
Care Management
Assess socioeconomic resources and access barriers; connect to support services.
Interprofessional Collaboration
Social work / community resources for support and access barriers; less partner (FOB) support is statistically common and should be screened for.
Safety
Increased risk for anemia, dystocia, preeclampsia, preterm birth, and impaired maternal-infant bonding — the adolescent's developmental stage (“feeling of invincibility”) can blunt risk perception, so reinforce warning signs concretely.

Delayed Childbearing (Age >35)

Pathophysiology
Advanced maternal age raises the rate and risk of pregnancy complications for both patient and fetus.
Assessment
Weigh advantages (financial resources, psychosocial maturity, self-confidence) against disadvantages (anxiety about antepartum testing, increased complication risk, exhaustion).
Teaching
Counsel on the purpose of increased antepartum surveillance and what to expect from testing.
Priority
Screening/prevention focus — alpha-fetoprotein, chorionic villus sampling, cell-free DNA, amniocentesis are used more heavily in this population (see Bonus section for detail).
Safety
Increased risk and rate of pregnancy complications compared with younger patients.

2. Hemorrhagic Conditions of Early Pregnancy

Watch on YouTubeComplications: Miscarriage, Hydatidiform Mole, Ectopic Pregnancy — Maternity Nursing | LevelUpRN

Spontaneous Abortion (Miscarriage)

Pathophysiology
Leading cause of pregnancy loss; most often chromosomal abnormalities incompatible with life. Rate increases with maternal age (older eggs).
Assessment
Types: threatened, inevitable, incomplete (needs misoprostol or D&C), complete, missed (needs misoprostol or D&C), recurrent (consider Rh incompatibility), and habitual abortion.
Priority
Preventing complications — hypovolemic shock and infection.
Care Management
Treatment options include D&C, prostaglandins, and Rhogam if the patient is Rh-negative.
Interprofessional Collaboration
Emotional/grief support is a core nursing responsibility alongside the medical plan.
Safety
Watch for signs of hemorrhage/shock and infection.

Cervical Insufficiency

Pathophysiology
Impaired cervical strength leads to premature, painless dilation of the cervix.
Assessment
Diagnosed from OB history of preterm births or late miscarriages; assess for vaginal bleeding.
Teaching
No intercourse, no prolonged standing, no heavy lifting; this recurs with every pregnancy and will be closely monitored/followed.
Priority
Strong emphasis on educating the patient to recognize signs/symptoms of preterm labor.
Care Management
Cervical cerclage — a “purse-string” suture placed at 12–14 weeks — is the management; removed around 37 weeks or with labor. Supportive care includes tocolytics, hydration, and bed rest.

★ Ectopic Pregnancy

★ High-yield — flagged in your Exam 2 Study Guide outline
Pathophysiology
Implantation of the fertilized ovum outside the uterine cavity; >95% occur in the fallopian tube. Incidence is rising, largely from pelvic inflammatory disease (PID).
Assessment
Severe/worsening unilateral abdominal pain, delayed menses, abnormal vaginal bleeding. Diagnosed via ultrasound and quantitative hCG levels.
Teaching
Contraception counseling is required after methotrexate — it is a chemotherapy drug, so the patient must wait at least 6 months before attempting pregnancy again to avoid teratogenic effects on a future pregnancy.
Priority
Recognize and prevent severe hemorrhage if the tube ruptures — this is an emergency.
Care Management
Medical management: methotrexate injection (given z-track). Surgical management if ruptured: salpingectomy (removal of the tube).
Interprofessional Collaboration
Fertility counseling/assistance may be needed given future fertility implications; follow-up care includes weekly hCG levels.
Safety
Risk factors: history of STI, PID, previous ectopic pregnancy, failed tubal ligation, IUD use, multiple induced abortions, maternal age >35, and use of assisted reproductive technologies.
Medication

Methotrexate

A chemotherapy drug given by injection to medically end an unruptured ectopic pregnancy without surgery, by stopping the rapidly dividing cells of the abnormal implantation from growing.

  • Medical (non-surgical) treatment for an unruptured ectopic pregnancy; given by z-track injection.
  • It is a chemotherapeutic/antineoplastic agent — teratogenic to a future pregnancy.
  • Patient counseling: avoid conceiving again for at least 6 months after the dose.
  • Follow-up: weekly quantitative hCG levels until they resolve to nonpregnant levels.

★ Gestational Trophoblastic Disease (Hydatidiform / Molar Pregnancy)

★ High-yield — flagged in your Exam 2 Study Guide outline
Pathophysiology
Abnormal cell growth of the trophoblast — a placenta develops but not a viable fetus (“gestational trophoblastic mole”).
Assessment
Diagnosed via ultrasound and hCG levels; risk of significant blood loss and/or anemia.
Priority
Ruling out malignant change (risk of uterine/gestational trophoblastic cancer).
Care Management
Management is evacuation/removal of the molar pregnancy (same approach as for a missed abortion/ectopic pregnancy).
Interprofessional Collaboration
Regular follow-up for one year: monitor hCG levels to confirm they are decreasing, with frequent pelvic exams.
Safety
Pregnancy should be avoided during the year of hCG surveillance.

3. Hemorrhagic Conditions of Late Pregnancy & Placental Variations

Watch on YouTubeAbruptio Placentae vs Placenta Previa — Nursing NCLEX Symptoms, Causes, Management

★ Placenta Previa

★ High-yield — flagged in your Exam 2 Study Guide outline
Pathophysiology
Placenta implants in the lower uterine segment, near or over the internal cervical os. Classified by how much of the os is covered: complete, marginal, or low-lying.
Assessment
Painless, bright-red uterine bleeding in the latter half of pregnancy, confirmed by ultrasound.
Priority
The placenta cannot deliver before the fetus — think about how the fetus would breathe/perfuse without the placenta. Watch for malpresentation, preterm birth, fetal anemia, and congenital anomaly risk.
Care Management
Expectant management (observation, bed rest) if bleeding is controlled; active management is cesarean birth. Be prepared for IV fluids, possible surgery/blood transfusion, and possible corticosteroids for fetal lung maturity if preterm delivery is anticipated.
Interprofessional Collaboration
OB/surgical team readiness for emergent cesarean and blood bank availability.
Safety
NO vaginal or rectal examinations (a digital exam can trigger catastrophic hemorrhage) and no intercourse — nothing per vagina.

★ Abruptio Placentae (Placental Abruption)

★ High-yield — flagged in your Exam 2 Study Guide outline
Pathophysiology
Premature separation of a normally implanted placenta before the fetus is born; graded mild, moderate, or severe. Bleeding forms a hematoma on the maternal side of the placenta and may be visible or concealed. Risk increases with cocaine use.
Assessment
Dark red vaginal bleeding (can be slight or absent if concealed), severe/persistent abdominal pain, uterine tenderness and hyperactivity. Signs of a CONCEALED hemorrhage: rising fundal height, hard board-like abdomen, high uterine baseline tone on the monitor strip, persistent abdominal pain, tachycardia with falling BP, restlessness, and persistent late decelerations in FHR. Also check whether membranes are ruptured (note fluid color) and cervical dilation.
Priority
High risk of fetal AND maternal mortality — recognize fetal distress and signs of hypovolemic shock early; this is an emergency.
Care Management
Emergency cesarean delivery; IV fluids, blood products, oxygen, continuous fetal and maternal monitoring.
Safety
Watch for progression to DIC — abruptio placentae is a known trigger.

Cord Insertion & Placental Variations

Assessment
Vasa previa: velamentous insertion of the cord (cord vessels run unprotected across/near the os) or a marginally-inserted cord. Succenturiate placenta: an accessory/additional placental lobe.
Safety
These variations raise the risk of fetal hemorrhage if the unprotected vessels rupture with membrane rupture or labor — recognized on ultrasound so delivery can be planned accordingly.

4. Hyperemesis Gravidarum

Watch on YouTubeComplications: Hyperemesis Gravidarum, Candidiasis, Anemia, UTI — Maternity Nursing | LevelUpRN

Hyperemesis Gravidarum

Pathophysiology
Persistent, uncontrollable nausea/vomiting beginning in the first weeks of pregnancy that may continue throughout, producing dehydration, electrolyte imbalance, ketosis, and acetonuria/ketonuria.
Assessment
Weight loss; monitor I&O and daily weight; watch for failure to thrive.
Priority
Correcting dehydration and electrolyte imbalance.
Care Management
Reduce nausea/vomiting with antiemetics (and pyridoxine per the presentation); maintain nutrition/fluid balance with IV fluids and small, frequent meals; possible corticosteroids.
Interprofessional Collaboration
Emotional support — this can be a prolonged, exhausting, isolating condition.
Safety
Monitor for signs of severe dehydration/electrolyte derangement requiring escalation of care.

5. Hypertensive Disorders of Pregnancy

Watch on YouTubeComplications: Preeclampsia, HELLP Syndrome, Amniotic Fluid Abnormalities — Maternity Nursing | LevelUpRN

Gestational Hypertension

Pathophysiology
New BP elevation after 20 weeks gestation without proteinuria.
Assessment
Systolic ≥140 mmHg or diastolic ≥90 mmHg, measured at least twice, 4+ hours apart, with NO proteinuria.
Priority
Close monitoring — may (not always) progress to preeclampsia.
Safety
If it persists more than 6 weeks after birth, it is reclassified as chronic hypertension.

Chronic Hypertension (pre-existing)

Pathophysiology
Hypertension that preceded the pregnancy or was present before 20 weeks gestation (before placental hormones “take over”) and persists after birth. No proteinuria.
Priority
Monitor closely for superimposed preeclampsia — “chronic hypertension with superimposed preeclampsia” is a sudden BP increase in a previously controlled patient, or new/worsening proteinuria.
Care Management
Antihypertensive medication is started if diastolic BP is >100 mmHg (may be discontinued if the patient is otherwise low risk); medications are adjusted to reduce teratogenic risk to the fetus. Antihypertensives may reduce milk supply, but have not been shown to harm the breastfeeding infant (unlike in-utero exposure).
Safety
Increased risk for placental abruption, superimposed preeclampsia, heart failure, acute kidney injury; fetal risks include IUGR, death, and preterm birth. Heredity and race play a significant role.

★ Preeclampsia

★ High-yield — flagged in your Exam 2 Study Guide outline
Pathophysiology
Generalized vasospasm decreases circulation/perfusion to all organs, including the placenta. Placental ischemia → more generalized vasospasm → increased BP, reduced kidney perfusion (oliguria/proteinuria), tissue edema, impaired liver function, neurologic complications, and risk of HELLP syndrome. Resolves after the placenta delivers, but risk continues for 6 weeks postpartum.
Assessment
HTN with proteinuria after 20 weeks gestation in a previously normotensive patient: systolic >140 mmHg or diastolic >90 mmHg with protein/creatinine ratio >0.3. Labs: liver enzymes, platelets, protein/creatinine ratio. Symptom assessment: headache, epigastric/RUQ pain, visual disturbances, generalized edema.
Priority
Early identification/prevention: low-dose aspirin therapy, early prenatal care, and patient education on reportable symptoms.
Care Management
Activity restriction (no strong evidence of benefit); fetal activity monitoring (kick counts, NSTs); BP monitoring at least twice weekly; weight measurement; urinalysis for protein and lab testing; low-sodium diet, limit caffeine; deep tendon reflex assessment; plan delivery close to 37 weeks EGA. For severe gestational HTN/severe preeclampsia: expectant management with perinatal specialists, antihypertensives, corticosteroids to prepare for early/preterm delivery, and magnesium sulfate with strict BP control once in labor.
Safety
Hyporeflexia precedes respiratory depression — always remember how to assess and document edema, and monitor for progression toward eclampsia.
Medication

Magnesium Sulfate

Given IV to prevent seizures in a patient with preeclampsia by calming neuromuscular excitability. It has a narrow safety margin, so respiratory rate and deep tendon reflexes are watched closely for early signs of toxicity.

  • Used to prevent seizures in labor for a preeclampsia patient — “neurological protection.”
  • Monitor respiratory rate and deep tendon reflexes to catch rising magnesium levels before they exceed the therapeutic level; hyporeflexia precedes respiratory depression.
  • Antidote: calcium gluconate — keep it readily available at the bedside whenever a patient is on IV magnesium sulfate, in case of toxicity.
  • Control external stimuli / initiate seizure precautions to protect the patient should preeclampsia progress to eclamptic seizure.
  • Always review your specific hospital's magnesium sulfate infusion and seizure protocols.

★ HELLP Syndrome

★ High-yield — flagged in your Exam 2 Study Guide outline
Pathophysiology
H = Hemolysis (fragmentation/distortion of RBCs passing through damaged vessels); EL = Elevated Liver enzymes (hepatic blood flow obstructed by fibrin deposits); LP = Low Platelets (vascular damage from vasospasm). More common in Caucasian women; increased risk of death and adverse perinatal outcomes.
Assessment
RUQ pain (liver location); reportable warning signs of worsening preeclampsia: general edema, sudden weight gain/loss, RUQ pain, headache unrelieved by acetaminophen.
Priority
Will deliver early — prepare for preterm delivery and maternal emergencies.
Safety
Associated with increased risk for pulmonary edema, acute renal failure, DIC, placental abruption, liver hemorrhage/failure, ARDS, sepsis, and stroke.

Eclampsia

Pathophysiology
Progression of preeclampsia to generalized seizures (and/or coma); the patient has no other seizure history. Can occur before, during, or after birth.
Priority
Immediate care during a seizure: maintain a patent airway, keep the patient safe, then stabilize after the seizure, continue/adjust magnesium sulfate, and assess fetal status.
Care Management
Prevention: initiate seizure precautions, monitor for signs of impending seizure, prevent seizure-related injury, and support the family — this is frightening to witness and is an obstetric emergency.
Safety
Continue to monitor for signs of magnesium toxicity during and after a seizure event.

6. Diabetes Mellitus in Pregnancy

Watch on YouTubeComplications: Gestational Diabetes Mellitus (GDM) — Maternity Nursing | LevelUpRN

Type 1 / Type 2 (pre-existing) Diabetes Mellitus

Pathophysiology
Type 1: disorder of carbohydrate metabolism; the body dilutes the increased glucose load through polydipsia/polyuria; insulin-dependent; protein metabolism results in ketosis. Type 2: may be diet-controlled or require insulin.
Safety
Patient risk: preeclampsia, UTIs (glucose spilling into urine), ketoacidosis, dystocia (from larger fetal size). Fetal risk: congenital malformations, macrosomia. Neonatal risk: hypoglycemia, hypocalcemia, hyperbilirubinemia, RDS.

★ Gestational Diabetes Mellitus (GDM)

★ High-yield — flagged in your Exam 2 Study Guide outline
Pathophysiology
First diagnosed during pregnancy; can be diet-controlled or insulin-controlled with diet. Early pregnancy metabolic needs change little; late pregnancy hormones create insulin resistance, leading to insufficient insulin, periods of hyperglycemia, and gluconeogenesis.
Assessment
Risk factors: overweight/obesity, maternal age >25, history of GDM, history of abnormal glucose tolerance, family history of diabetes, high-risk ethnic group. All pregnant patients are screened: glucose challenge test at 24–28 weeks (no fasting needed; glucose drink, draw at 1 hour). If failed (or high-risk), glucose tolerance test: fasting, then blood drawn before the drink and at 1, 2, and 3 hours after.
Teaching
Teach the patient to recognize hypoglycemia (shakiness, sweating, pallor, cool skin, disorientation, headache, hunger, blurred vision) versus hyperglycemia (fatigue, flushed skin, dry mouth, excessive thirst, frequent urination, rapid/fruity-smelling breathing, drowsiness, headache, decreased reflexes).
Priority
Goal is maintaining blood glucose in range — a large fetus from poor glucose control raises the risk of birth injury and neonatal hypoglycemia.
Care Management
Diet is first-line (least invasive) — dietician/diabetes educator consult; exercise improves glucose metabolism; glucose monitoring fasting and 2 hours postprandial; fetal surveillance (NSTs) starting at 28 weeks; additional ultrasounds to track fetal size. Oral medications (glyburide, metformin) may be tried before insulin if diet/exercise aren't enough. In labor: continuous glucose monitoring, insulin may be given IV.
Safety
✱ Insulin and Pitocin (oxytocin) are NOT compatible in the same IV line during labor.
Medication

Oxytocin (context only — not a full drug review in these sources)

Not covered as a full drug review in Week 4 — it's noted here only because it can't share an IV line with insulin, and because it's the same agent used to trigger contractions for a contraction stress test. The full induction/augmentation review is in Week 5.

  • Noted as incompatible with insulin when both are needed during labor — keep on separate lines.
  • Also referenced (Bonus deck) as the agent used for an oxytocin-stimulated contraction stress test.

7. Cardiac Disease in Pregnancy

Watch on YouTubeHeart Disease and Pregnancy

Cardiac Disease in Pregnancy

Pathophysiology
Normal pregnancy already increases cardiovascular workload; in a patient with preexisting heart disease this added burden can cause cardiac decompensation. Types noted: rheumatic heart disease (post-streptococcal valve scarring/stenosis), congenital heart disease (left-to-right or right-to-left shunt), and cardiomyopathy (including peripartum/postpartum cardiomyopathy).
Assessment
Recognize signs/symptoms of heart failure: shortness of breath, generalized edema. NYHA-style classification used: Class 1 (asymptomatic, no activity limitation) and Class 2 (symptomatic with heavy exertion) carry small maternal/fetal risk; Class 3 (symptomatic with minimal exertion) and Class 4 (symptomatic at rest) are greatly compromised.
Priority
Primary goal of management is preventing the development of heart failure.
Care Management
Intrapartum and postpartum management focuses on preventing fluid overload, which stresses the heart. Medications may include anticoagulants, anti-dysrhythmics, anti-infectives, and heart-failure drugs. Cesarean may be considered to reduce the cardiac stress of labor exertion. Intrapartum management is tailored to the patient's specific symptoms/presentation.
Safety
Give additional assessment and attention to any pregnant patient with known heart disease.

8. Clotting Disorders — Disseminated Intravascular Coagulation (DIC)

Watch on YouTubeDIC (Disseminated Intravascular Coagulation) NCLEX® Review

Disseminated Intravascular Coagulation (DIC)

Pathophysiology
Normal clotting is a balance between hemostatic and fibrinolytic systems. In DIC (a “consumptive coagulopathy”), pro-coagulation and anti-coagulation factors activate simultaneously and ineffectively — the body tries to clot and bleed at the same time. Triggered by missed abortion, abruptio placentae, severe preeclampsia, HELLP, and gram-negative sepsis.
Assessment
Assess for symptoms of bleeding everywhere and signs of shock.
Priority
Major emergency. The priority in treating DIC is delivery of the fetus and placenta — the underlying cause must be treated. Act FAST.
Care Management
Blood replacement products and cryoprecipitate to maintain circulating volume; start a second IV line; monitor I&O (watch for renal failure from DIC); constant reassessment.
Interprofessional Collaboration
Call a rapid response — recognize the symptoms early and prepare to perform a massive blood transfusion with the team.
Safety
Recognize this can progress extremely fast; be ready to act.

9. Anemias in Pregnancy

Watch on YouTubeUnderstanding Iron Deficiency Anemia in Pregnancy

Iron-Deficiency Anemia

Pathophysiology
Causes 75% of anemias in pregnancy; hemodilution of pregnancy compounds the effect. Difficult to meet pregnancy iron needs through diet alone.
Assessment
Signs: shortness of breath, fatigue, pallor, pica. Monitor H&H and ferritin.
Teaching
Prenatal vitamins (not gummies), iron-rich foods, increase vitamin C to aid absorption; educate that iron supplements affect stool (darkening/constipation).
Safety
Watch for maternal, fetal, and neonatal effects of uncorrected anemia.

Folic Acid Deficiency (Megaloblastic Anemia)

Pathophysiology
Associated with increased risk for neural tube defects.
Care Management
Supplementation is necessary — prenatal vitamins.
Safety
Watch for maternal, fetal, and neonatal effects of deficiency.

Sickle Cell Anemia

Pathophysiology
Autosomal recessive disorder causing abnormal hemoglobin, leading to RBC distortion and destruction; pregnancy worsens the condition and results in poor perfusion to both patient and fetus.
Priority
Primary goal is preventing sickle cell crisis during pregnancy — this is an emergency when it occurs.

Thalassemia

Pathophysiology
Genetic disorder involving abnormal synthesis of the alpha and beta hemoglobin chains; labs resemble iron-deficiency anemia.
Safety
High risk — pregnancy is only advised if hemoglobin is at least 10 g/dL and cardiac function is adequate.

10. Other Medical Complications

Surgery During Pregnancy

Assessment
Two conditions named in the sources requiring surgical consideration during pregnancy: appendicitis, and cholelithiasis/cholecystitis.

Immune Complex Diseases

Pathophysiology
Systemic lupus erythematosus (SLE): chronic, inflammatory autoimmune disease that can affect any organ/system. Antiphospholipid syndrome: a cluster of conditions associated with increased risk for thrombosis, fetal loss, and low platelets. Hashimoto's thyroiditis: chronic lymphocytic thyroiditis.

Epilepsy (not eclampsia)

Pathophysiology
Generalized seizures are the most common form of epilepsy. This is a distinct condition from eclampsia — different underlying cause, different treatment — though seizure precautions still apply to both.
Care Management
Management is complicated by the teratogenic effects of anticonvulsant medications; risk vs. benefit must be weighed, but these patients must stay on their medications.
Safety
Generalized seizures can cause fetal hypoxia/acidosis and increase the risk of cerebral palsy.

11. Infections During Pregnancy

Watch on YouTubeGBS (Group B Streptococcus) in Pregnancy | Maternity Nursing

Cytomegalovirus (CMV)

Pathophysiology
Droplet transmission; the patient sheds large amounts of virus in saliva, urine, and respiratory secretions.
Safety
Congenital CMV: jaundice, low birth weight, enlarged/poorly-functioning liver, pneumonia, seizures.

★ Rubella

★ High-yield — flagged in your Exam 2 Study Guide outline
Pathophysiology
Transmitted via the placenta; associated with poor fetal outcomes.
Teaching
Educate to avoid infected individuals while pregnant. Vaccinate before pregnancy, or postpartum if not vaccinated prior.
Priority
Prevention is best — rubella CANNOT be given in pregnancy (it's a live vaccine).

Varicella-Zoster & Herpes Simplex Virus (Type 1 & 2)

Pathophysiology
HSV Type 1 causes oral lesions, Type 2 causes genital lesions; transmission to the neonate is possible at birth.
Care Management
If active lesions are present at the time of delivery, plan for cesarean section.
Safety
Neonatal effects: lethargy, poor feeding, jaundice, pneumonia, bleeding, seizures, bulging fontanelles, lesions — high mortality rate.
Medication

Acyclovir

An antiviral given during pregnancy to suppress herpes outbreaks so active lesions aren't present at delivery, lowering the chance the newborn is exposed during a vaginal birth.

  • Given in pregnancy to prevent active herpes lesions from being present at the time of delivery.
  • If active lesions ARE present at delivery despite treatment, the delivery plan shifts to cesarean section to reduce neonatal transmission risk.

Parvovirus

Assessment
Named in the sources as an infection of concern in pregnancy (detailed teaching points were not covered yet in these materials).

Hepatitis B

Priority
Transmission to the newborn at birth can be prevented with immediate prophylaxis: vaccine and immune globulin (IG) at birth.

HIV

Pathophysiology
A retrovirus that gradually reduces the effectiveness of the patient's immune system.
Care Management
Treat the pregnant patient with zidovudine (ZDV) along with other antiviral medications — this substantially reduces transmission to the fetus. The newborn should be started on ZDV within 6–12 hours of birth.
Safety
No breastfeeding. Monitor for complications.

Group B Streptococcus (GBS)

Pathophysiology
Leading cause of life-threatening perinatal infections; raises risk of chorioamnionitis in labor.
Assessment
Culture/swab checked at approximately 36 weeks EGA.
Care Management
Patient should receive a minimum of 2 rounds of IV antibiotics (penicillin or ampicillin) in labor to decrease risk to the fetus. A cesarean section performed without rupture of membranes (ROM) does not require GBS treatment.
Safety
Can cause neonatal sepsis, pneumonia, and meningitis.

Tuberculosis

Assessment

12. Amniotic Fluid & Membrane Complications

Watch on YouTubeChorioamnionitis

Polyhydramnios

Assessment
Excess amniotic fluid; possible associated fetal anomalies. Associated risk: gestational diabetes.

Oligohydramnios

Assessment
Too little amniotic fluid. Consider uteroplacental insufficiency or fetal genitourinary abnormalities as underlying causes.

PPROM / PROM (Preterm) Premature Rupture of Membranes

Care Management
Consider amnioinfusion as a management option.
Safety
Raises risk of chorioamnionitis and preterm birth — assess fluid color/odor whenever a patient reports her water broke.

Chorioamnionitis

Pathophysiology
Infection of the amniotic sac.
Assessment
Fever, elevated WBCs, uterine tenderness, foul-smelling fluid/odor.
Care Management
Will need antibiotics.
Safety
Risk of sepsis to both the pregnant patient and the fetus/newborn.

13. Multiple Gestation

Multiple Gestation / Multifetal Pregnancy

Pathophysiology
Identical twins result from one fertilized ovum splitting; fraternal twins result from two separately fertilized ova.
Assessment
Determine whether each fetus has its own sac and placenta; assess presentation of each fetus.
Priority
Close monitoring throughout pregnancy; all fetuses must be cephalic for a vaginal delivery to be attempted.
Safety
Larger overall size raises risk of hemorrhage, gestational diabetes, preterm labor, PROM, and growth restriction.

Categories of Risk Factors

Assessment
Biophysical risks (factors originating within mother or fetus affecting development/function of either); psychosocial risks (maternal behaviors and adverse lifestyle); sociodemographic risks (arising from the mother and her family); environmental factors (workplace/general environment hazards).

Biophysical Risk Factors (examples from the deck)

Assessment
Genetic disorders, defective/transmissible inherited disorders and chromosomal anomalies, multiple gestation, large fetal size, ABO incompatibility, nutritional/general health status, adolescent pregnancy, three pregnancies within two years, substance use/abuse, inadequate dietary intake, inadequate/excessive weight gain, hematocrit <33%, poorly controlled diabetes mellitus, hypertensive disorders.

Psychosocial, Sociodemographic & Environmental Risk Factors

Assessment
Psychosocial: disturbed interpersonal relationships, emotional distress, inadequate social support, unsafe cultural practices, substance abuse, history of mental illness. Sociodemographic: low income, lack of prenatal care, age (adolescent or older mother), parity, marital status, ethnicity. Environmental: workplace hazards (chemicals, anesthetic gases, radiation, pollutants), infection, substance use/abuse.

Biophysical Assessment — Daily Fetal Movement Count (DFMC)

Care Management
Simple, valuable method to evaluate fetal condition. Methods: once daily for 60 minutes, OR 2–3 times daily after meals for 2 hours each, counting toward 10 movements in a 12-hour period.

Biophysical Assessment — Ultrasonography

Assessment
Levels: standard, limited, specialized. Indications: fetal heart rate activity, gestational age, fetal growth, fetal anatomy, fetal genetic disorders/physical anomalies, placental position and function, adjunct to other invasive tests, Doppler blood flow analysis, amniotic fluid volume, biophysical profile / modified biophysical profile.
Teaching
Nursing role includes counseling and educating about the procedure. Note: AIUM and ACOG strongly discourage nonmedical (souvenir) 3-D/4-D ultrasounds.

Biophysical Assessment — MRI

Assessment
Used to evaluate fetal structure/growth, the placenta (position, density, presence of gestational trophoblastic disease), amniotic fluid quantity, maternal structures (uterus, cervix, adnexa, pelvis), and biochemical status of tissues/organs.

Biochemical Assessment — Amniocentesis

Assessment
Indications: genetic concerns, women over 35, family history of chromosomal abnormalities, fetal maturity (L/S and S/A ratios), fetal hemolytic disease.
Safety
Maternal complications: hemorrhage, fetomaternal hemorrhage, infection, labor, abruptio placentae, damage to intestines/bladder, amniotic fluid embolism. Fetal complications: death, hemorrhage, infection (amnionitis), needle injury. Risks are minimized by using ultrasound guidance during the procedure.

Biochemical Assessment — Chorionic Villus Sampling (CVS)

Assessment
Performed between 10 and 13 weeks gestation; removes a small tissue specimen from the fetal portion of the placenta (chorionic villi originate in the zygote, so tissue reflects fetal genetic makeup).
Priority
Offers earlier diagnosis and more rapid results than amniocentesis.

Biochemical Assessment — Percutaneous Umbilical Blood Sampling (PUBS)

Assessment
Direct access to fetal circulation via needle insertion into a fetal umbilical vessel under ultrasound guidance. In many centers this has been replaced by placental biopsy.

Maternal Serum Assays

Assessment
Alpha-fetoprotein (AFP): screens for neural tube defects (detects 80–85% of open NTDs and abdominal wall defects early); recommended for all pregnant patients. Multiple marker screens: detect chromosomal abnormalities, including increased risk for trisomy 21. Coombs' test: detects Rh incompatibility and other antibodies against maternal antigens. Cell-free DNA screening: newest noninvasive prenatal genetic test; detects fetal Rh status, fetal gender, and paternally-transmitted single-gene disorders by amplifying cell-free DNA.

Antepartal Assessment — Electronic Fetal Monitoring

Assessment
Goal: determine whether the intrauterine environment is supportive to the fetus. Nonstress test (NST): fetal heart rate + contraction monitor; “reactive” = 2 accelerations of 15 beats x 15 seconds, normal FHR, and moderate variability within 20 minutes. Vibroacoustic stimulation may be used. Contraction stress test (CST): nipple-stimulated or oxytocin-stimulated contractions; provides a warning of fetal compromise earlier than the NST.

Psychosocial Considerations in High-Risk Pregnancy

Assessment
Patients may feel stress related to a high-risk diagnosis: low self-esteem, guilt, frustration; this may alter the ability to attach to the fetus.
Interprofessional Collaboration
The nurse's role is to provide encouragement and support throughout the careful monitoring these pregnancies require.

1. Dysfunctional Labor (Dystocia)

Watch on YouTubeLabor Dystocia, Prolapsed Umbilical Cord, Cesarean Section — Maternity Nursing | LevelUpRN

Dystocia — the 4 P's

Pathophysiology
Prolonged, difficult labor from a problem in one of four areas. Powers: ineffective contractions or ineffective pushing (secondary arrest, arrest of descent, an epidural that's “too heavy”), or uterine overdistension (polyhydramnios) causing hypertonic (sinusoidal) or hypotonic labor dysfunction. Passenger: fetal anomalies, cephalopelvic disproportion (CPD), malposition/malpresentation (most commonly occiput posterior), or multifetal pregnancy. Passage: pelvis shape/size, maternal soft tissue obstruction, or patient position. Psyche: a perceived threat from fear, pain, or the situation triggering a fight-or-flight stress response that itself can slow labor.
Assessment
Hands-and-knees positioning can help reposition an OP fetus and relieves lower back pressure. With multifetal pregnancy, once the first fetus delivers, the second often has more room to change position (possible vaginal delivery of fetus A and cesarean of fetus B).
Care Management
Augmentation is the treatment when the patient is in labor but not progressing.
Safety
Unexpected labor duration cuts both ways: prolonged labor risks maternal/neonatal infection, maternal exhaustion, and higher anxiety/fear; precipitate labor (birth within 3 hours of labor onset) carries a higher risk of hemorrhage.

★ Precipitous Labor

★ High-yield — flagged in your Exam 2 Study Guide outline
Pathophysiology
A rapid birth occurring within 3 hours of the onset of labor — often becomes a “nurse delivery.”
Priority
Stay with the patient and call for help immediately — do not leave to find someone else. Know your scope of practice: don't try to prevent or encourage an unattended delivery, but always be prepared for one.
Care Management
Encourage a side-lying position, apply O2, start/continue IV fluids, and discontinue oxytocin if it's running. Glove up. Provide gentle counter-pressure to the fetal head to help control (not stop) the pace of delivery.
Safety
Increased risk of maternal lacerations and trauma, postpartum hemorrhage, and fetal intracranial hemorrhage, bruising, and hyperbilirubinemia.

2. Membrane & Amniotic Fluid Complications

★ PROM / PPROM (Premature Rupture of Membranes)

★ High-yield — flagged in your Exam 2 Study Guide outline
Pathophysiology
PROM: rupture of membranes before the onset of labor — labor will likely be induced. PPROM: preterm premature rupture of membranes, occurring before 37 weeks, from pathologic weakening of the amniotic membranes (inflammation, stress from uterine contractions).
Priority
Infection is the greatest risk with both PROM and PPROM.
Care Management
PPROM is managed conservatively (least invasive interventions first); the patient is usually hospitalized, sometimes for weeks.
Safety
Watch for signs of chorioamnionitis developing.

★ Chorioamnionitis

★ High-yield — flagged in your Exam 2 Study Guide outline
Pathophysiology
Bacterial infection of the amniotic cavity; a major cause of complications and more common in preterm births.
Assessment
Clinical findings: maternal fever, fetal tachycardia, uterine tenderness, foul odor of the amniotic fluid.

★ Polyhydramnios & Obesity in Pregnancy

★ High-yield — flagged in your Exam 2 Study Guide outline
Pathophysiology
Polyhydramnios (excess amniotic fluid) causes uterine overdistension, which is one of the “powers” causes of dysfunctional labor. Obesity is an increasingly serious problem in pregnancy — patients are more likely to begin pregnancy with preexisting HTN or DM, and have increased risk of post-date pregnancy and its complications.
Care Management
Obesity creates physical challenges to nursing care: positioning the patient and obtaining an accurate fetal heart rate can both be more difficult.

3. Obstetric Procedures: Version, Induction & Augmentation

Watch on YouTubeBirth Procedures (AROM, Meconium, Pitocin, Vacuum, Forceps) for Nursing NCLEX

Version (Repositioning a Malpositioned Fetus)

Assessment
External cephalic version: ultrasound-guided manual turn to vertex, done after 37 weeks and before labor. Internal version is emergent, done during labor; cesarean follows if unsuccessful.
Care Management
Get a reactive NST before the procedure; terbutaline is administered beforehand; assess the patient before, during, and after, and monitor patient and fetus for 1–2 hours (LD presentation says a minimum of 2 hours) post-procedure. Labor may be induced following a successful version.
Safety
Contraindicated with abnormal placenta placement, a previous cesarean with a vertical/T incision, active herpes lesions, oligohydramnios, a non-reactive NST, or multifetal gestation. Risk: the fetus becomes tangled in the cord.

Induction of Labor (IOL)

Assessment
Elective induction (not medically indicated) is done at 39+ weeks. Medically indicated induction: HTN, preeclampsia, diabetes, post-dates/term, or a suspected fetal problem such as IUGR. Contraindicated with placenta previa, cord prolapse, fetal malposition, active herpes lesions, or a previous non-low-transverse cesarean.
Priority
Assess cervical readiness with the Bishop score before inducing — a score of at least 6 is considered favorable/more favorable for labor. Obtain an NST before starting, and monitor continuously during induction.
Care Management
Cervical ripening methods: chemical agents (misoprostol — note the dose differs from the hemorrhage-control dose — and Cervidil/dinoprostone), mechanical/physical methods (Foley bulb with traction, taped to the leg or attached to a liter of fluids; the nurse may be ordered to tug on the bulb), and alternative methods (nipple stimulation). Amniotomy (AROM) is performed by the provider, who keeps a hand in the birth canal to confirm the fetal head comes down onto the cervix and there is no prolapsed cord; monitor FHR before, during, and after (cord prolapse risk). The nurse assesses fluid using “TACCO”: Time, Amount, Color, Consistency, Odor.
Interprofessional Collaboration
Augmentation of labor (AROM and/or oxytocin) is used when labor progress stops or slows; amnioinfusion (0.9% NS, preferably warmed, through an IUPC) is used to reduce variable decelerations — follow facility protocol, document fluid in and out, and watch for uterine rupture/distension risk.

★ Oxytocin (Pitocin) for Induction/Augmentation

★ High-yield — flagged in your Exam 2 Study Guide outline
Pathophysiology
A hormone normally produced by the posterior pituitary gland that stimulates uterine contractions; used to induce labor or augment labor that is progressing slowly from an ineffective contraction pattern.
Priority
HIGH-ALERT medication with many risk factors: increased risk for placental abruption, uterine rupture, unplanned cesarean, postpartum hemorrhage, infection, and fetal hypoxemia / acidemia. Must have a reactive NST before starting.
Care Management
Administered IV, hung along with LR; titrated closely per facility policy and EBP guidelines based on uterine activity, aiming for strong contractions every 2–3 minutes with regular cervical dilation. Dosed in milliunits per minute. Half-life is 5 minutes. It is NEVER hung as a secondary line — it goes in its own chamber on an IV pump, attached to the port closest to the patient. Requires constant fetal monitoring.
Safety
In uterine resuscitation (“Flip, Fluid, Flow”), turning off the Pitocin is one of the resuscitative interventions for a non-reassuring FHR pattern.
Medication

Oxytocin (Pitocin)

A synthetic hormone used to start or strengthen labor contractions. Because it can overstimulate the uterus, it's treated as a high-alert medication requiring continuous fetal monitoring and its own dedicated IV line.

  • High-alert drug for labor induction/augmentation — risks include placental abruption, uterine rupture, unplanned cesarean, postpartum hemorrhage, infection, fetal hypoxemia/acidemia.
  • Requires a reactive NST before starting and continuous fetal monitoring throughout.
  • IV with LR, in its own pump chamber on the port closest to the patient — never as a secondary line.
  • Titrated to achieve contractions every 2–3 minutes with regular cervical dilation; half-life 5 minutes.
  • “Flip, Fluid, Flow” uterine resuscitation: turning off the oxytocin is one of the first steps for a non-reassuring FHR pattern.
  • Not compatible in the same line as insulin (see Week 4 GDM content).

4. Operative & Assisted Vaginal Birth

★ Forceps-Assisted Birth

★ High-yield — flagged in your Exam 2 Study Guide outline
Priority
Monitor and document the time forceps are applied.
Care Management
Episiotomy is common. The provider pulls along with the patient's push effort/contractions.
Safety
Increased trauma risk to the patient (vaginal lacerations, hematoma) and to the fetus (scrapes, bruising, increased risk of hyperbilirubinemia/jaundice).

★ Vacuum-Assisted Birth

★ High-yield — flagged in your Exam 2 Study Guide outline
Teaching
Both forceps- and vacuum-assisted birth are used for fetal distress or a prolonged second stage; make sure the bladder is empty first. An assisted vaginal birth is usually faster than a cesarean.
Priority
Monitor and document the time the vacuum is applied.
Care Management
Pressure should be relieved between contractions/pulls; the provider pulls with the patient's pushing effort. Limit: 2 pop-offs.
Safety
Less traumatic for the patient but significantly riskier for the newborn: cephalohematoma, subgaleal/intracranial hemorrhage, hyperbilirubinemia, fetal death.

5. Cesarean Birth

Watch on YouTubeCesarean delivery [ C-Section ] | Nursing Lecture

Cesarean Birth — Indications & Types

Assessment
Indications: mechanical obstruction (fibroids), history of previous cesarean (with VBAC contraindicated/undesired), nonreassuring FHR/pattern, malpresentation or malposition, active herpes, congenital anomalies, dysfunctional labor (arrest of labor, CPD), placental abruption or previa, uterine rupture. Scheduled: usually no labor prior, spinal anesthesia. Unplanned (urgent/emergent): typically laboring first; an existing epidural may be dosed up to a spinal, but a true emergency requires general anesthesia.
Care Management
Surgical technique is low-transverse or vertical (classical) incision.
Safety
Complications/risks: infection, pneumonia, DVT, hemorrhage, shock.

★ Cesarean Perioperative Nursing Care

★ High-yield — flagged in your Exam 2 Study Guide outline
Teaching
Additional postoperative care: anesthesia typically manages pain for the first 24 hours; turn-cough-deep-breathe and incentive spirometer; advance diet slowly; ambulate within 4 hours (standing at the bedside, marching in place); foley should be discontinued within 6 hours.
Priority
The highest priority after a cesarean is monitoring the patient for hemorrhage and hypovolemic shock.
Care Management
Preop: NPO, informed consent, labs, monitor patient and fetus, IV, foley, bilateral SCDs, prophylactic antibiotic within 30 minutes of incision, Bicitra PO to neutralize gastric acid. Intraop: the L&D nurse typically circulates — performs counts before/during/after and assists anesthesia. Immediate postop: the L&D nurse recovers the patient (general anesthesia may mean initial recovery in PACU); vital signs q5min x5 then regular postpartum trends, q4h maximum until discharge.
Safety
The INITIAL dressing is removed ONLY by the surgeon — not the nurse, not the aide, not the student. If the site is bleeding, keep adding pads and call the surgeon; do not remove the dressing. Once the surgeon has removed it, the nurse can assess the incision (always checking for infection: warm, red, draining).

Trial of Labor / VBAC

Assessment
Trial of labor (TOL): observing the patient and fetus for a specified time to assess the safety of a vaginal birth. VBAC candidacy: the indication for the primary cesarean (e.g. dystocia, breech, fetal distress) is often non-recurring, and a prior low-transverse incision makes a patient a candidate.
Safety
Increased risk for uterine rupture during a trial of labor after cesarean.

6. Preterm Labor

Watch on YouTubeCommonly used tocolytics drugs; medications used to delay preterm labor

Preterm Labor — Prediction & Prevention

Pathophysiology
Labor beginning after 20 weeks but before 36+6 weeks gestation; the exact cause is unknown.
Assessment
Predicting: cervical length via ultrasound; fetal fibronectin (a protein secreted by fetal membranes, not normally present until after 36 weeks — if present earlier, it indicates preterm labor risk). Warning signs: cramping, low/dull backache, pelvic pressure, leaking fluid (PPROM), general feeling unwell.
Teaching
Prevention: education for both the pregnant patient and partner, improved access to care, progesterone supplementation, adequate nutrition. Increased frequency of prenatal visits for at-risk patients supports early detection.
Care Management
Initial measures to stop preterm birth: identify/prevent/treat infections, limit activity (including sexual activity), hydration, and tocolytics. Fetal surveillance with NSTs and BPP.
Interprofessional Collaboration
Counseling and education by the interprofessional team, including the neonatologist.

★ Tocolytics

★ High-yield — flagged in your Exam 2 Study Guide outline
Pathophysiology
Medications used to decrease contractions or stop labor.
Care Management
Agents used: terbutaline, magnesium sulfate (here for fetal neurological protection — unrelated to its preeclampsia use), nifedipine (a calcium channel blocker), and indomethacin (inhibits prostaglandins). Betamethasone steroid injections are given alongside to accelerate fetal lung maturity (surfactant production) in case delivery can't be stopped.
Safety
As a class, tocolytics can cause respiratory depression.
Medication

Terbutaline

A tocolytic injection that relaxes uterine muscle to stop or slow contractions — used both to delay preterm labor and, briefly, to relax the uterus right before an external cephalic version.

  • Tocolytic — an injection that relaxes the uterus to stop contractions; given SubQ.
  • Vasodilator — the patient will feel hot/flushed; also causes tachycardia and heart palpitations.
  • Also given before an external cephalic version to relax the uterus for the procedure.

Medication

Magnesium Sulfate

In preterm labor, this same drug is given for a different reason than in Week 4 — here it protects the preterm fetus's developing brain (neuroprotection), not to prevent maternal seizures.

  • In preterm labor, given for fetal neurological protection — this use is unrelated to its role in preeclampsia/eclampsia (see Week 4).

Betamethasone

8. Intrapartum Emergencies

Watch on YouTubeUterine Rupture, Amniotic Fluid Embolism, Precipitous Labor — Maternity Nursing | LevelUpRN

Placental Abnormalities (Accreta Spectrum & Vasa Previa)

Assessment
Placenta accreta, increta, and percreta are all abnormal placental attachment that embeds beyond the wall of the uterus. Vasa previa involves velamentous cord insertion.
Safety
The shared risk across these is hemorrhage progressing to shock.

★ Shoulder Dystocia

★ High-yield — flagged in your Exam 2 Study Guide outline
Pathophysiology
The head delivers but the anterior shoulder cannot pass under the pubic arch. Classic sign: “turtling.”
Priority
Verbalize and document the time passing — more than 5 minutes raises risk of fetal asphyxia. This is a team emergency: you will need help, including the neonatal team.
Care Management
The nurse assists with the McRoberts maneuver and suprapubic pressure.
Safety
Newborn risk: birth injuries, especially to the brachial plexus. Maternal risk: hemorrhage, rectal injuries.

★ Umbilical Cord Prolapse

★ High-yield — flagged in your Exam 2 Study Guide outline
Pathophysiology
The umbilical cord slips down/out before (or alongside) the fetus after membranes rupture, and becomes compressed between the fetus and the pelvis.
Assessment
FHR pattern: variable decelerations progressing to a prolonged deceleration.
Priority
Priority is promoting perfusion / prompt delivery — this is a crash cesarean situation. “Whoever finds it, rides it”: do NOT move your hand. Call for help — you are not leaving the room.
Care Management
Use a gloved sterile hand in the vagina to push the fetal presenting part upward and off the cord. Position the patient knee-chest, or elevate the hips / use Trendelenburg, to use gravity to shift the fetus off the cord. If the cord is exposed, cover it with gauze soaked in sterile normal saline and apply oxygen. Goal: keep the presenting part off the cord and expedite delivery.
Safety
Risk factors: ruptured membranes, presenting part at high station or malpresentation, a fetus that fits the pelvic inlet poorly, polyhydramnios (excess fluid), a long cord.

Uterine Rupture

Pathophysiology
Most frequently occurs at the scar of a previous cesarean or uterine surgery.
Assessment
Sharp/severe abdominal pain and tenderness, possible chest or shoulder-area pain, abnormal FHR (often sinusoidal), loss of fetal station, palpable fetal parts outside the uterus, and signs of shock (tachycardia, hypotension, pallor).
Care Management
Administer IV fluids and blood as ordered; emergency cesarean delivery, with possible hysterectomy.
Safety
Complications: maternal hemorrhage, fetal hypoxia, death of either patient. Continue monitoring for hypovolemic shock.

Uterine Inversion

Pathophysiology
The uterus completely or partly turns inside out, usually during the third stage of labor — uncommon but potentially fatal.
Care Management
Recovery care focuses on promoting uterine contraction and maintaining adequate circulating volume.
Safety
Often accompanied by massive blood loss and shock.

Anaphylactoid Syndrome of Pregnancy (Amniotic Fluid Embolism)

Pathophysiology
Amniotic fluid enters the maternal circulation and obstructs the pulmonary vessels / is carried to the lungs, usually at delivery or immediate recovery.
Assessment
Sudden chest pain, SOB, tachycardia, hypotension, bleeding, progressing to acute hypoxia, cardiovascular collapse, and coagulopathy (can result in DIC).
Priority
Rapid management is critical — assist with CPR, intubation, and mechanical ventilation; correct hypotension; start a second IV; prepare for blood transfusion.
Safety
Maternal mortality is 61% or higher; diagnosis is typically made post-mortem (autopsy detects amniotic fluid in the lungs); neonatal outcome is also poor.

1. Classifying the High-Risk Newborn

High-Risk Neonate — Classification

Pathophysiology
A high-risk neonate is any newborn, regardless of gestational age or birth weight, with a greater-than-average chance of morbidity or mortality from conditions or circumstances associated with birth and the adjustment after birth. Classified by birth weight/gestational age (SGA, LGA, preterm, post-term) and by birth trauma.
Assessment
Ultrasound allows antepartum diagnosis of macrosomia, hydrocephalus, and unusual presentations; an elective cesarean may be chosen for some pregnancies specifically to prevent significant birth injury.

Care of the High-Risk Newborn & Family — “S.T.A.B.L.E.”

Teaching
Discharge planning covers whether gavage feedings or oxygen therapy will continue at home and what equipment is needed. Many facilities offer a “trial room” for 1–2 nights where the family provides all care with nurses just outside the door, to build bonding and confidence before discharge.
Care Management
When in doubt, keep them S.T.A.B.L.E.: thermoregulation (thermal stability, incubator, kangaroo care for bonding), respiratory support (establish/maintain respirations, supplemental O2, assisted ventilation), protection from infection (standard precautions), hydration, nutrition (breastfeeding/breastmilk, transitioning to oral feeds via bottle or gavage), and energy conservation (cluster care, skin care).
Interprofessional Collaboration
Include the family in care, involve Child Life Specialists when available, and track developmental outcomes against the infant's corrected gestational age.
Safety
NICU families are at high risk for impaired bonding — offer kangaroo care whenever possible, and give the parent privacy while doing it.

2. Birth Trauma / Birth Injuries

Watch on YouTubeCaput Succedaneum vs. Cephalohematoma | Newborn Head Assessment | OB Nursing

★ Caput Succedaneum vs. Cephalohematoma

★ High-yield — flagged in your Exam 2 Study Guide outline
Assessment
Caput succedaneum CROSSES the suture line (from compression of blood vessels causing diffuse scalp edema/molding). Cephalohematoma does NOT cross the suture line (a subperiosteal bleed confined by the skull suture). Also watch for subgaleal hemorrhage, and petechiae/abrasions/edema/bruising.
Safety
Cephalohematoma / subgaleal hemorrhage raise the risk of hyperbilirubinemia as the trapped blood breaks down.

★ Skeletal & Peripheral Nerve Birth Injuries

★ High-yield — flagged in your Exam 2 Study Guide outline
Assessment
Skeletal: e.g. fractured clavicle. Erb-Duchenne (Erb's) palsy: lack of arm movement, abnormal arm position, diminished sensation, asymmetrical Moro reflex, and asymmetrical grip/strength on the affected (upper body) side. Facial nerve paralysis: absence of movement on the affected side, especially noticeable when the infant cries — greater risk with forceps delivery. Phrenic nerve (diaphragm) paralysis is rare, from hyperextension of the neck at birth, and can cause respiratory distress.
Priority
Almost all Erb's palsy fully recovers. Facial nerve paralysis is not considered permanent and can take weeks to recover. Phrenic nerve paralysis needs surgery if it doesn't resolve on its own.

3. The Preterm Infant

Watch on YouTubePreterm and Postmature Infants, Meconium Aspiration Syndrome — Maternity Nursing | LevelUpRN

★ Preterm Infant — Characteristics

★ High-yield — flagged in your Exam 2 Study Guide outline
Pathophysiology
Birth between 20 and 36+6 weeks (late preterm: 34–36+6 weeks); most organ systems are immature. Many risk factors are recognized but the ultimate cause is often unknown: maternal gestational HTN, cervical incompetence, amnionitis, history of preterm delivery, uterine abnormalities, PPROM, placenta previa.
Assessment
Appearance: small/scrawny with minimal subcutaneous fat, large head, poor tone, extra lanugo (unless very early), extra vernix, absent/few sole creases, thin/translucent “jelly-like” skin, possible fused eyelids in micro-premies. Behavior: easily exhausted by noise and outside activity.
Safety
Increased risk for problems with thermoregulation, hypoglycemia, hyperbilirubinemia, sepsis, and respiratory function.

★ Preterm Infant — Respiratory & Thermoregulation

★ High-yield — flagged in your Exam 2 Study Guide outline
Pathophysiology
Surfactant production is not complete until approximately 36 weeks, so the immature respiratory system carries a real risk of RDS; poor cough reflex, narrow airway, and weak muscles add to this. Heat loss is also a major concern: thin skin, little/no brown fat, and a large surface area relative to body mass.
Care Management
Positioning prone or side-lying increases oxygenation and reduces energy loss/use. Maintain the infant in a warming device and monitor temperature continuously; micro-premies are often wrapped in plastic wrap at delivery to prevent heat loss and protect the skin. Administer O2/humidification as prescribed with continuous O2 sat monitoring.
Medication

Betamethasone (antenatal, for the fetus at risk of preterm birth)

★ Preterm Infant — Fluid, Nutrition & Overstimulation

★ High-yield — flagged in your Exam 2 Study Guide outline
Pathophysiology
Preterm infants lose fluid easily (thin skin, increased RR, radiant warmers) and their kidneys can't concentrate urine well. They lack nutrient reserves, so glucose reserves are used fast (risk of hypoglycemia), and they need more protein and minerals than a term infant while having an immature digestive system with an uncoordinated suck-and-swallow reflex.
Care Management
Feeding: gavage or pre-nipple feeding until the infant shows readiness (positive gag reflex, stable vital signs, coordinated suck/swallow) to nipple feed; monitor for fatigue. Breast milk is especially beneficial for preterm infants and helps protect against necrotizing enterocolitis — educate on its immunologic benefits and easier digestibility, and support pumping.
Interprofessional Collaboration
Extended NICU stays put parents at risk for delayed bonding. Signs to watch for: using negative/impersonal terms, not using the infant's name, infrequent visiting/calling that subsides over time, refusing to hold/care for the infant, less time talking/smiling at the infant. Orient parents to the NICU, use therapeutic communication, involve them in care, and encourage kangaroo care.
Safety
Too much stimulation increases oxygen expenditure. Watch for oxygen-change signs (unstable vitals, cyanosis/pallor, flared nares, sneezing, coughing, retractions, grunting) and physical stress signs (stiff extremities, arching back, hiccupping, gagging, regurgitation, frowning). Cluster care and reduce stimuli to protect the infant's limited reserves.

Respiratory Distress Syndrome (RDS)

Pathophysiology
A serious respiratory disorder from lung immaturity and surfactant deficiency, resulting in hypoxia and acidosis.
Assessment
Risk factors: preterm birth, IUGR, PPROM, maternal diabetes, maternal HTN, maternal drug use, neonatal sepsis. S/s: absent breathing/crying at birth, nasal flaring, intercostal retractions, audible grunting, cyanosis, tachypnea, hypothermia, poor muscle tone.
Care Management
Treatment: surfactant, oxygen therapy, CPAP, mechanical ventilation, nitric oxide (relaxes pulmonary blood vessels), orogastric feedings or TPN. NICU admission. Nursing: monitor color, respiratory rate/effort and ABGs; percussion and vibration respiratory therapy; support nutrition and bonding (encourage pumping).
Safety
Any preterm infant needing oxygen support should have an eye exam before discharge to check for retinal damage — retinopathy of prematurity is the primary risk with supplemental oxygen beyond 30 days.

Bronchopulmonary Dysplasia (BPD)

Pathophysiology
A pulmonary condition affecting newborns who experienced respiratory failure and/or were oxygen-dependent.
Assessment
Diagnosis: X-ray shows areas of overinflation and atelectasis. S/s: respiratory distress, difficulty feeding, apnea, expiratory wheezing, decreased air movement.
Care Management
Treatment: CPAP, ventilation, oxygen, possible fluid restriction. Nursing: monitor airway and cardiopulmonary function; small, frequent feedings with increased calories.
Medication

BPD medications: diuretics, bronchodilators, caffeine, corticosteroids

A combination of medications used to manage bronchopulmonary dysplasia (chronic preterm lung disease): diuretics reduce fluid buildup, bronchodilators open the airways, and caffeine and corticosteroids support lung function and breathing.

  • Diuretics (furosemide) to manage fluid.
  • Bronchodilators (albuterol) to open the airway.
  • Caffeine and corticosteroids are also used as part of BPD management.

★ Necrotizing Enterocolitis (NEC)

★ High-yield — flagged in your Exam 2 Study Guide outline
Pathophysiology
An inflammatory disease of the GI mucosa from ischemia, resulting in necrosis and possible bowel perforation.
Assessment
Risk factors: preterm, polycythemia, myelomeningocele. S/s: feeding intolerance, abdominal distension, lethargy, vomiting, bloody stools.
Priority

4. Post-Term & Growth-Restricted / Overgrown Infants

★ Post-Term Infant

★ High-yield — flagged in your Exam 2 Study Guide outline
Pathophysiology
Born after 42+0 weeks. The placenta is only designed to function for about 40 weeks, so it starts to calcify/age, causing decreased perfusion (fetal hypoxia).
Assessment
S/s: long hair/nails; parchment-like, dry/cracked/peeling skin; loose, wrinkled, leathery skin; loss of subcutaneous fat; no vernix or lanugo; possible meconium staining; hypoglycemia (can't regulate blood sugar); inadequate thermoregulation.
Safety
Complications: fetal hypoxia, birth injury risk rises because these infants tend toward LGA as they keep growing.

Meconium Aspiration Syndrome

Pathophysiology
Meconium-stained (green) amniotic fluid is inhaled into the lungs at birth; meconium is thick and sticky, so it obstructs the alveoli, impairs gas exchange, and can inactivate surfactant.
Assessment
Risk factors: post-term infant, fetal hypoxia, oligohydramnios, placental insufficiency (recurrent late decelerations). S/s: green-stained amniotic fluid, dyspnea at birth.
Priority
At delivery, prepare everything for neonatal resuscitation. If the newborn is delivered active and crying with no respiratory distress, no intervention is needed. If the newborn shows poor tone and no cry, endotracheal suctioning is performed; if there's no respiratory effort and a low heart rate, NRP is needed. Do not stimulate the infant at birth if meconium aspiration is suspected before the airway is managed.
Care Management
Complications: airway obstruction, surfactant inactivation, pneumonia. Severe cases may benefit from ECMO (a modified heart-lung machine that oxygenates the blood, letting the lungs rest and decreasing pulmonary hypertension/hypoxemia) — this is a very sick baby. Nursing: monitor blood glucose (hypoglycemia), maintain temperature, provide early/frequent feedings, monitor for dehydration and hypoxemia.

★ Small for Gestational Age (SGA)

★ High-yield — flagged in your Exam 2 Study Guide outline
Pathophysiology
Fetal growth restriction; below the 10th percentile at birth.
Priority
Priority nursing focuses: temperature and blood sugar (both marked as priority in your instructor's slides).
Care Management
Frequent, early feedings.
Safety
Associated risk factors include maternal smoking and drug use.

★ Large for Gestational Age (LGA) / Macrosomia

★ High-yield — flagged in your Exam 2 Study Guide outline
Pathophysiology
Greater than the 90th percentile, or weighing more than 4000 g. Associated with gestational diabetes and maternal obesity.
Assessment
Birth injury risks: fractures, nerve damage, bruising. Metabolic risks: hypoglycemia/polycythemia, hyperbilirubinemia.
Priority
Priority nursing focuses: temperature and blood sugar (both marked as priority in your instructor's slides).
Care Management
Frequent, early feedings; assess for dehydration. Hypoglycemia happens because the infant is taken out of the high-glucose maternal environment (“all-you-can-eat buffet, then cut off”) it adapted to in utero.

Infant of a Diabetic Mother

Pathophysiology
Born to a mother with type 1, type 2, or gestational diabetes; presents as LGA/macrosomic from excessive fat and glycogen in the tissues.
Care Management
Early and frequent blood glucose testing (this applies to both LGA infants and any infant of a diabetic mother, regardless of diabetes type): check within the first 30 minutes of life, then every 1–3 hours before feedings for 24 hours or until “3 good ones in a row,” per facility policy. Watch for rebound hypoglycemia as the infant adjusts from a higher glucose “intake” in utero.
Safety
Complications: birth injuries (asphyxia, shoulder dystocia), respiratory distress, hypoglycemia, polycythemia, hypocalcemia, hypomagnesemia, hyperbilirubinemia. Increased risk for congenital anomalies.

★ Neonatal Hypoglycemia

★ High-yield — flagged in your Exam 2 Study Guide outline
Assessment
Defined as serum glucose <40 mg/dL in the first 4 hours of life, or <45 mg/dL from 4–24 hours after delivery. Greatest risk: preterm/post-term infants, infants of diabetic mothers, and perinatal stress. S/s: poor feeding, jitteriness, weak cry, fatigue, flaccid/poor tone, seizures, diaphoresis, cyanosis, apnea, irritability.
Care Management
Treat least-invasive to most-invasive: early initiation of breastfeeding, supplementation (formula or donor human milk), dextrose gel, then IV glucose. Nursing: frequent feedings, keep the infant warm, and monitor glucose closely with heel sticks.
Medication

Dextrose gel / IV glucose

  • Used to treat neonatal hypoglycemia, escalating from least to most invasive: breastfeeding first, then supplementation, then dextrose gel, then IV glucose if still not resolving.

5. Hemolytic Disorders (Pathologic Jaundice)

★ Rh Isoimmunization

★ High-yield — flagged in your Exam 2 Study Guide outline
Pathophysiology
Only affects Rh-positive infants born to Rh-negative mothers. The mother can form antibodies against fetal blood cells, attacking and destroying them (erythroblastosis fetalis), causing hemolytic anemia or hyperbilirubinemia. Progressive hemolysis causes fetal hypoxia and can lead to hydrops fetalis (cardiac failure, generalized edema, stillbirth).
Assessment
Labs: indirect Coombs test checks the mother's blood for antibodies; direct Coombs test checks the baby's blood for antibodies already attached to the cells.
Priority
The second (or later) pregnancy is at risk once the mother is sensitized.
Care Management
Rhogam is given to the mother if she is not yet sensitized; a sensitized pregnancy/infant will need transfusion (intrauterine if needed).
Safety
Risk of kernicterus and encephalopathy if hyperbilirubinemia goes unmanaged.
Medication

Rhogam

An injection given to an Rh-negative mother to prevent her immune system from forming antibodies against Rh-positive fetal blood — it only works as prevention, before sensitization has already occurred.

  • Given to an Rh-negative mother who is not yet sensitized, to prevent her from forming antibodies against Rh-positive fetal blood cells.
  • Prevention is the goal — a sensitized patient instead requires transfusion (intrauterine if needed) for the affected pregnancy.

★ ABO Incompatibility

Pathophysiology
Most common as maternal type O blood with a fetal blood type of A or B.
Assessment
Unlike Rh disease, jaundice can appear as early as the first pregnancy, and it progressively worsens with each subsequent pregnancy.

6. Neonatal Abstinence Syndrome & Prenatal Substance Exposure

Watch on YouTubeNeonatal Abstinence Syndrome Simulation

★ Neonatal Abstinence Syndrome (NAS)

★ High-yield — flagged in your Exam 2 Study Guide outline
Pathophysiology
Most often due to opioid use during pregnancy; the infant has been exposed in utero and must be weaned.
Assessment
High-pitched/shrill cry, tremors, convulsions, fever, tachypnea, diaphoresis (notable because newborns aren't otherwise supposed to sweat), irritability, poor feeding, diarrhea, constant sucking, increased muscle tone. Behavior can look similar to hypoglycemia (irritable, jittery, tremors, muscle rigidity, restless, difficult to console, poor sleep); feeding issues include excess/uncoordinated sucking, frequent regurgitation, diarrhea, weight loss; also nasal stuffiness, sneezing, tachypnea, apnea, retractions, grunting, fever, diaphoresis, mottling. Symptoms may be delayed.
Priority
Perform NAS/Finnegan scoring every 3 hours — the higher the score, the worse the infant is doing (asking: can they eat, sleep, be consoled?). Seizure precautions.
Care Management
Nursing: decrease stimuli, cluster care with minimal handling, frequent small high-calorie meals, swaddle/soothe (5 S's, “cuddler” volunteers are very beneficial), perform NAS screening every 3 hours.
Interprofessional Collaboration
Refer the biological mother to a treatment/support center as needed; involve a CPS/social services consult.
Safety
Narcan (naloxone) is CONTRAINDICATED in infants born to opioid-dependent patients — it can trigger severe NAS symptoms and seizures.
Medication

NAS weaning medications

Medications used to gradually wean an opioid-exposed newborn off dependence and control withdrawal symptoms. Narcan (naloxone) must NOT be used here — reversing the opioid too fast can trigger severe withdrawal and seizures.

  • Morphine sulfate, buprenorphine, and methadone are used to wean the infant off opioid exposure.
  • Anticonvulsants (phenobarbital) are added if needed, including for benzodiazepine withdrawal (which can appear 1–3 weeks postpartum).
  • Narcan (naloxone) is CONTRAINDICATED — it can precipitate severe withdrawal and seizures in these infants.

Other Prenatal Substance Exposures

Assessment
Tobacco: low birth weight from nicotine-induced vasoconstriction and fetal hypoxemia; increased risk of congenital anomalies, miscarriage, abruptio placentae, placenta previa, preterm birth. Alcohol: Fetal Alcohol Spectrum Disorders (fetal alcohol syndrome is most severe) — cognitive/physical delays, hearing disorders; assess short upturned nose, flat midface, thin upper lip, low nasal bridge, abnormal palmar creases, respiratory distress, congenital heart disorders, tremors, irritability, poor feeding, seizures. Marijuana: the most common illicit drug used by pregnant patients in the US; THC crosses the placenta easily, raising risk of preterm birth, stillbirth, and IUGR. Cocaine: risk of miscarriage, preterm birth, abruptio placentae, PROM, death, decreased fetal oxygenation (vasoconstriction), congenital anomalies. Methamphetamine: risk of placental abruption, preterm birth, IUGR; withdrawal shows agitation, tremors, hypertonia, poor feeding. SSRIs: infants can show withdrawal (hypertonic, tremors, high-pitched crying, feeding problems, pulmonary hypertension) — weigh maternal benefit vs. infant risk, and monitor closely if breastfeeding.
Care Management
Alcohol-exposed infant nursing care: monitor, assess suck/swallow reflex, give small frequent feedings, monitor weight/height/head circumference, decrease stimuli. Cocaine-exposed infants follow the same nursing care as NAS infants.

7. Neonatal Sepsis & TORCH Infections

Neonatal Sepsis

Pathophysiology
A generalized bloodstream infection in an infant less than 28 days old.
Assessment
Risk factors: prematurity, low birth weight/SGA, chorioamnionitis, prolonged rupture of membranes. S/s: poor feeding, irritability, lethargy, respiratory distress, temperature instability (fever OR hypothermia — often low, around 95°F or below), hypotension, pallor, cyanosis, tachypnea, tachycardia, abdominal distention, GI upset, sunken fontanels (dehydration); worsening signs include jaundice, petechiae/purpura (bleeding), anemia, enlarged liver/spleen, respiratory failure, shock, seizures. Labs: positive blood cultures, hyper- or hypoglycemia (often starts high then drops), acidosis.
Priority
Prevention is always the main priority; obtain labs/cultures before starting antibiotics.
Care Management
Treatment is IV antibiotics. Nursing: assess and alert the provider to early s/s, watch for apnea (O2 support), monitor I&Os and weight, assess for jaundice, obtain specimens as ordered.

TORCH Complex Infections

Assessment
Congenital infections that can cause complications: Toxoplasmosis (can affect the newborn's brain, eyes, heart, kidneys, blood, liver, or spleen), Hepatitis B, HIV, Parvovirus, West Nile, Rubella, Cytomegalovirus, Herpes simplex virus, and Zika.

8. Congenital Anomalies

★ Cleft Lip and/or Palate

★ High-yield — flagged in your Exam 2 Study Guide outline
Pathophysiology
Occurs when tissues don't fuse together during fetal development. Cleft lip: opening at the lip. Cleft palate: opening in the roof of the mouth into the nasal cavity. Risk increases with smoking, gestational diabetes, use of steroids/other medications, and lack of folate in pregnancy.
Assessment
Can be visualized on prenatal ultrasound and assessed visually at birth.
Teaching
Treatment is surgery: lip repair within 3–5 months, palate repair within 12 months, with follow-up surgeries into the teen years as the palate grows. Post-op: avoid pacifiers (can disrupt the suture line) and use elbow immobilizers/restraints to protect the surgical site.
Care Management
Feeding: a special elongated nipple; for a cleft lip, squeeze the cheeks together during feeding; for a cleft palate, feed in an upright position with a one-way-flow valve nipple and burp frequently.
Safety
A major complication is inadequate feeding — the infant can't achieve proper suction, making it very difficult to pull milk/formula from a standard nipple. Cleft palate carries a significant aspiration risk because of the opening into the nasal cavity.

Esophageal Atresia (EA) & Tracheoesophageal Fistula (TEF)

Pathophysiology
EA: the upper esophagus does not connect to the lower esophagus/stomach. TEF: the esophagus and trachea are abnormally connected. Risk factor: polyhydramnios.
Assessment
S/s: choking, respiratory distress during feeding, cyanosis, abdominal distension. Diagnosed with x-ray or bronchoscopy.
Care Management
These infants must have surgery for repair. Preop nursing: maintain a patent airway, suction to prevent aspiration, NPO, IV fluids, support the parents.

★ Omphalocele & Gastroschisis

★ High-yield — flagged in your Exam 2 Study Guide outline
Pathophysiology
Omphalocele: the defect is covered by the peritoneum and goes through the umbilicus. Gastroschisis: the bowel is exposed (not covered), off to the side of the umbilicus.
Assessment
Diagnosed via ultrasound before delivery; a cesarean section is planned if the anomaly is known ahead of time.
Care Management
Treatment: surgery, IV fluids, antibiotics. Nursing: cover at minimum the exposed organs with a sterile (moist) covering to protect and reduce heat/moisture loss; an OG tube is ordered to decompress the abdomen.
Safety
Major risk of infection. Complications: short bowel syndrome, necrotizing enterocolitis.

★ Neural Tube Defects / Myelomeningocele / Spina Bifida

★ High-yield — flagged in your Exam 2 Study Guide outline
Pathophysiology
Neural tube defects are the largest group of congenital anomalies. Spina bifida/myelomeningocele is often associated with hydrocephalus.
Assessment
Diagnosis: MRI, ultrasound, CT; elevated alpha-fetoprotein in amniotic fluid at 16–18 weeks gestation; chorionic villus sampling can be done after 10 weeks EGA.
Priority
Prevention: folic acid during pregnancy. “KNOW SPINA BIFIDA” — taking folic acid is the key prevention point your instructor flagged.
Care Management
Treatment: surgery, with orthopedic considerations and management of GU/GI function; prognosis depends on the specific defect and any associated anomalies.
Safety
Position the infant PRONE and avoid any trauma to the lesion; maintain the integrity of the sac with sterile gauze.

★ Hydrocephalus

★ High-yield — flagged in your Exam 2 Study Guide outline
Pathophysiology
An imbalance in the production and absorption of CSF — impaired CSF absorption in the subarachnoid space, or obstruction through the ventricular system (communicating vs. noncommunicating). Often associated with myelomeningocele; can also result from neoplasms, infection, or trauma.
Assessment
Assess head circumference (track/monitor over time), bulging fontanels, separated sutures, dilated scalp veins, lethargy/irritability/change in LOC. Later in infancy: “setting sun” sign, sluggish/unequal pupillary response.
Care Management
Treatment focuses on managing complications; surgical treatment is a ventriculoperitoneal (VP) shunt. Assess for problems related to motor development.

★ Developmental Dysplasia of the Hip (DDH)

★ High-yield — flagged in your Exam 2 Study Guide outline
Pathophysiology
The ball-and-socket joint of the hip doesn't form properly, leading to hip instability that can progress to full dislocation (the femoral head displaces, shortening the affected leg). Typically presents at birth or in the first few years.
Assessment
Risk factors: breech position, family history, female sex. S/s: leg length asymmetry, asymmetrical inguinal/gluteal (thigh) skin folds, limited hip range of motion. After 12 weeks: limited hip abduction and a shortened leg on the affected side. Walking years: pelvic tilt and “Trendelenburg sign.” Diagnosis (provider only): positive Barlow and Ortolani maneuvers, ultrasound, x-ray.
Care Management
Treatment: Pavlik harness for infants under 6 months; surgical reduction with a hip spica cast for children over 6 months. Nursing: neuro and skin checks, cast care.
Safety
Only the HCP adjusts the harness straps. The harness stays on 100% of the day; dress and diaper the infant UNDER the straps, avoid powders/lotions, and check skin 2–3 times a day. Never put the legs straight or together — position (“saddle”) the infant with hips abducted and flexed.

Clubfoot (Talipes Equinovarus)

Pathophysiology
A bone deformity where one or both feet are twisted out of shape/position, pointing down and inward with the soles facing each other, from genetic/environmental factors causing a shortened Achilles tendon.
Care Management
Treatment: casting soon after birth; a long-leg cast is recast every week for 5–8 weeks for gradual repositioning; surgery if casting doesn't correct it.
Safety
Never place these infants on their stomachs to sleep — they are high risk for SIDS since the cast limits their ability to roll over/adjust. Keep the cast dry; do not elevate the feet on a pillow to sleep; check color/warmth and perform neuro and pulse checks several times a day.

9. Newborn Screening for Disease

★ Phenylketonuria (PKU)

★ High-yield — flagged in your Exam 2 Study Guide outline
Pathophysiology
A rare genetic disorder (both parents must be carriers) in which the body lacks the enzyme phenylalanine hydroxylase, needed to break down the amino acid phenylalanine into tyrosine. Unconverted amino acids build up and can cause permanent, severe brain damage / neurological impairment.
Priority
Caught through newborn screening — early dietary management is what prevents the brain damage.
Care Management
Management is diet: the infant needs a special low-phenylalanine formula and diet. Fruits and vegetables are best; avoid high-protein and high-iron foods (meat, eggs, dairy, peanut butter); tyrosine in the diet is fine/good.

Other Newborn Screening Findings

Assessment
Congenital hypothyroidism: protruding thick tongue, dull look, swollen face, slowed reflexes, decreased tone, hoarse cry, abdominal distension, low thyroid labs. Galactosemia: identified via genetic evaluation/counseling. Trisomy 18: not compatible with life — infants die in utero or shortly after birth. Trisomy 21 (Down syndrome): developmental delays (physical and mental); s/s include cardiac murmur, flat nasal bridge, small nose/mouth/ears, short stocky stature, low-set ears.

10. Neonatal Loss

Neonatal Loss — the Nurse's Role

Teaching
Tangible mementos are important: photographs, handprints/footprints, a lock of hair, bedside name card, ID bracelet/armband.
Care Management
Prepare parents for an inevitable death; provide end-of-life care for the infant and family; facilitate the grieving process. Offer the family the chance to hold the infant before and be present at the time of death; “cold/cooling cribs” are often available so the infant can remain with the family for a short while after death.
